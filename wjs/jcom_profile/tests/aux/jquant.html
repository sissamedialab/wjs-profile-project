ABSTRACT

The study of Entanglement Asymmetry has emerged in recent years as a powerful tool to characterise the symmetry properties of quantum states in relation to a given charge operator through the lens of entanglement. While extremely powerful and general, the standard definition of asymmetry introduces significant non-Gaussian features in free-fermionic systems, leading to certain analytical limitations. In this work, we introduce an asymmetry measure that remains strictly within the Gaussian manifold and analyse its properties. In particular, we show that it quantifies the minimal distance between a Gaussian state and the manifold of symmetric Gaussian states. We further demonstrate that this measure captures the established dynamical signatures of entanglement asymmetry, such as the Mpemba effect, symmetry restoration, and the lack thereof. The Gaussian structure allows these novel asymmetry measures to be computed exactly using correlation matrix techniques, and to be described asymptotically through the quasiparticle picture. We also comment on the possibility of using charge fluctuations to characterise the asymmetry of a Gaussian state.

1 Introduction

The characterisation of the symmetry properties of quantum states has attracted considerable interest in recent years. While global symmetries are relatively easy to characterise, through the conserved charges of the system, and in general fix the properties of ground and equilibrium states, the symmetry properties of general dynamical quantum systems are much more subtle to capture. For example, even though the Mermin-Wagner theorem forbids spontaneous symmetry breaking of continuous symmetries in the ground state, a general quantum state ρ\rho evolving under a Hamiltonian HH that commutes with a U(1)U(1) charge generated by an operator QQ can nevertheless exhibit strong symmetry breaking. This may occur either at the level of the full system, [ρ,Q]≠0[\rho ,Q] \neq 0, or at the level of subsystems, [ρA,QA]≠0[\rho _A, Q_A] \neq 0, where the reduced density matrix is defined as ρA = TrA¯[ρ]\rho _A = \operatorname {Tr}_{\overline {A}}[\rho ]. Considering properties of the local density matrix has been of fundamental importance in the last decades, as it has allowed to understand the mechanisms underlying quantum thermalisation, mostly through the lens of entanglement [1–7]; hence it is natural to study symmetries from the same point of view, as it allows to make general thermodynamic and hydrodynamic (transport related) statements.

It is therefore of great theoretical importance to have a diagnostic tool to detect the presence or absence of a given symmetry and, in the latter case, to quantify the degree of its breaking at the level of the subsystem density matrix ρA\rho _A. While a natural first approach would be to consider charge fluctuations within AA, this naive strategy fails, as the distribution of charge fluctuations in out-of-equilibrium systems is highly nontrivial even for symmetric states [8, 9]. A more effective quantifier, termed entanglement asymmetry (EA), has been introduced in recent years [10–13]: focusing on a U(1)U(1) symmetry with generator Q = ∑ iqiQ = \sum _i q_i, this is defined as the relative entropy between the local state ρA\rho _A and its symmetrisation ρA,Q\rho _{A,Q}

ΔS(ρA) = S(ρA||ρA,Q) = S(ρA,Q) −S(ρA) (1.1)\begin {equation} \Delta S(\rho _A) = S(\rho _A||\rho _{A,Q}) =S(\rho _{A,Q}) - S(\rho _A) \end {equation}

where ρA,Q = ∑ qPqρPq\rho _{A,Q} = \sum _q P_q \rho P_q is obtained by twirling over the U(1)U(1) group generated by QAQ_A (i.e., projecting onto definite local charge sectors). Being a relative entropy, ΔSA \Delta S_A vanishes if and only if [ρA,QA] = 0 [\rho _A, Q_A] = 0 , namely if the state is locally symmetric; moreover, it constitutes a good measure of symmetry breaking as it minimises the relative entropy of ρ\rho with any symmetric state [10]. Reintroduced in the context of many-body quench dynamics [13], entanglement asymmetry has rapidly emerged as a versatile diagnostic of nonequilibrium symmetry restoration and its absence, enabling the identification of counterintuitive relaxation phenomena, known as the quantum Mpemba effect (QME) [13–17] (by analogy with a famous classical effect [18]) according to which states which exhibit stronger symmetry breaking at initial times can restore such symmetry faster. Such effect has been subject of an intensive investigation, allowing to observe it in free and integrable systems [19–29], free systems with linear dissipation [30] or continuous Gaussian measurements [31], quantum circuits [32–40], random models [41, 42], long-range systems [43–45], and chaotic models [46–51]. Beyond the insight on dynamical phenomena, the EA has also been characterised in ground states [52–56] and quantum field theories [57–69], general bounds on its value has been provided [70–72] and its typical behavior in random states has been analised [73–77].

While the EA has the advantage of being extremely general, its application to the study of symmetry restoration in free-fermionic systems may appear somewhat odd. In fact, considering a fermionic Gaussian state evolving via a quadratic Hamiltonian, the evolution at all times is restricted to the manifold of Gaussian states. On the other hand, ρA,Q\rho _{A,Q} is a highly non-Gaussian state, meaning that the dynamics of ρA\rho _A lies within a set of states which are completely disconnected from it, except for asymptotically large times, when the the symmetry is restored together with Gaussianity (up to pathological situations [47, 78, 79]). It is therefore natural to ask whether one can assess the symmetry properties of the state without leaving the Gaussian manifold. Such an approach would allow one to study symmetry restoration by measuring the distance to a set of states that are, in principle, dynamically connected to ρ\rho . Beyond its conceptual appeal, this approach would also be of significant analytical value, as it would allow the study of asymmetry using only correlation-matrix techniques [80]. As is well known, within the standard formulation of EA these methods only provide access to the charged moments [13]. In contrast, remaining within the Gaussian manifold would open the way to closed-form analytical expressions for the large space-time dynamics of the asymmetry, based on the quasiparticle picture [81–84], which is known to emerge in a broad class of free quenches from Gaussian states [8, 85–94]. In this work we face precisely this problem, and introduce a measure of asymmetry which lies within the Gaussian manifold. We then show that this framework can be used as a diagnostic for both the Mpemba effect and the absence of symmetry restoration. In both cases, it yields simple, asymptotically exact results within the quasiparticle picture, allowing for an immediate criterion for the occurrence of the Mpemba effect that shares the same semplicity as the one in ref. [21]. Finally, we comment on the idea to use charge fluctuations as a probe of symmetry breaking.

2 Some notions on Gaussian states

We first briefly introduce the main ideas regarding Gaussian states which will be used in the following. A (fermionic) Gaussian state is the ground, excited, or thermal state of a quadratic hamiltonian. Equivalently, it is a state in which all multi-point correlation functions can be reduced to the two-point functions, which can be encoded in the correlation matrix

C = ( G F F† 1 − GT ) (2.1)\begin {equation} C = \begin {pmatrix} G && F\\F^\dag && 1 -G^T \end {pmatrix} \end {equation}

where G = ⟨ci†cj⟩ i,j=1,…LG = \braket {c_i^\dag c_j}_{i,j=1,\ldots L} and F = ⟨cicj⟩ i,j=1,…LF = \braket {c_i c_j}_{i,j=1,\ldots L} represent the normal and pairing correlators respectively, cic_i and ci†c^\dag _i being lattice spinless fermions defined by the canonical anticommutation relations {ci,cj†} = δi,j\{c_i,c_j^\dag \}=\delta _{i,j} (the approach is straightforwardly generalised to continuous fermions [95], but we focus here on lattices for simplicity). Since a Gaussian state is fully determined by the correlation matrix CC, we will refer to it as ρC\rho _C. Reduced density matrices for any subsystem AA are also Gaussian, and obtained from the restriction CAC_A of the correlation matrix to the subsystem. When restricting to the subsystems, the relation between the state and the correlation matrix can be understood in terms of the Entanglement Hamiltonian (EH) defined as ρA = eKA∕ZA\rho _A = e^{K_A}/Z_A [96–99], which can be expressed through the relation [100, 101]

(kA)ij = log⁡(1 −CA−1) ij (2.2)\begin {equation} (k_A)_{ij} = \log (1-C_A^{-1})_{ij} \end {equation}

where (kA)ij(k_A)_{ij} are the matrix elements of the EH in the natural fermionic basis.

It is easy to show that for a Gaussian state described by a given correlation matrix CAC_A, [ρA,QA] = 0⟺FA = 0[\rho _A,Q_A] = 0 \iff F_A=0. The ⇒\Rightarrow implication is immediately obtained by considering the properties of Tr[ρAcicj]\Tr [\rho _A c_i c_j] under rotations of ρ\rho induced by the group elements: by the symmetry, we have

Tr[ρAcicj] = Tr[eiαQA ρAe−iαQA cicj] = e2iα Tr[ρ Acicj] (2.3)\begin {equation} \Tr [\rho _A c_i c_j] = \Tr [e^{i\alpha Q_A} \rho _A e^{-i\alpha Q_A} c_i c_j ] = e^{2i\alpha } \Tr [\rho _A c_i c_j] \end {equation}

where we have used cyclicity of the trace and e−iαQAcieiαQA = eiαcie^{-i\alpha Q_A} c_i e^{i\alpha Q_A} = e^{i\alpha }c_i; the equivalence can thus only be satisfied if FA = 0F_A=0 identically. The opposite implication comes instead from the consideration that if FA = 0F_A=0 the Entanglement Hamiltonian is of the form KA = ∑ x,y∈Akxycx†cy K_A=\sum _{x,y \in A} k_{xy} c_x^\dag c_y [101] and therefore commutes with QAQ_A, which immediately implies that ρA\rho _A commutes with QAQ_A. It is important to note that the condition [ρA,QA] = 0[\rho _A, Q_A] = 0 represents a weak form of symmetry, as it does not imply that ρA\rho _A is an eigenstate of the charge operator. Indeed, the reduced density matrix is typically mixed, and for this reason even a (weakly) symmetric state of this kind can exhibit nontrivial charge fluctuations within the subsystem AA.

Given a non-Gaussian state ρA\rho _A, it is always possible to Gaussianise it: this is simply done by constructing the (unique) Gaussian state ρCA\rho _{C_A} from the correlation matrix CAC_A of ρA\rho _A. This allows to introduce a natural measure of Non-Gaussianity [102–104]

NG(ρ) = S(ρA||ρCA) = S(ρCA) −S(ρA). (2.4)\begin {equation} {\rm NG}(\rho ) = S(\rho _A||\rho _{C_A}) = S(\rho _{C_A}) - S(\rho _A). \end {equation}

This is a good measure, as it minimises the distance between ρA\rho _A and the manifold of Gaussian states [105]. It is possible to highlight an interesting relation between non-Gaussianity and asymmetry: given a Gaussian state ρCA\rho _{C_A} defined by a correlation matrix

CA = ( GA FA FA† 1 −GAT ) , (2.5)\begin {equation} \label {eq:CA} C_A = \begin {pmatrix} G_A && F_A\\F_A^\dag && 1 -G_A^T \end {pmatrix}, \end {equation}

its symmetrisation ∑ qPqρCAPq \sum _q P_q \rho _{C_A} P_q is a strongly non-Gaussian state. However the symmetrisation acts on the correlation matrix in a simple way,

Tr [(∑ qPqρCAPq) ci†c j] = Tr [ρCA (∑ qPqci†c jPq)] = Tr[ρCAci†c j] Tr [(∑ qPqρCAPq) cicj] = Tr [ρCA (∑ qPqcicjPq)] = 0, (2.6)\begin {equation} \begin {split} \Tr \left [\left ( \sum _q P_q \rho _{C_A} P_q \right )c_i^\dag c_j\right ] &= \Tr \left [ \rho _{C_A} \left ( \sum _q P_q c_i^\dag c_j P_q\right )\right ] = \Tr [ \rho _{C_A} c_i^\dag c_j ] \\ \Tr \left [ \left (\sum _q P_q \rho _{C_A} P_q\right ) c_i c_j\right ] &= \Tr \left [ \rho _{C_A} \left ( \sum _q P_q c_i c_j P_q\right )\right ] = 0, \end {split} \end {equation}

namely, the normal part is unvaried while the pairing part is set to zero. Therefore, the Gaussianisation of the symmetrised state is simply a Gaussian state ρC A(s) \rho _{C_A^{(s)}} identified by the correlation matrix

CA(s) = ( GA 0 0 1 −GAT ) , (2.7)\begin {equation} C_A^{(s)} = \begin {pmatrix} G_A && 0\\0 && 1 -G_A^T \end {pmatrix}, \end {equation}

which, as shown above, is a symmetric state. It is therefore natural to introduce the notion of Gaussian symmetrisation, defined as an operation that maps a generic Gaussian state ρCA\rho _{C_A} to a Gaussian state ρC A(s)\rho _{C_A^{(s)}} by directly symmetrising its correlation matrix. We stress that this Gaussian symmetrisation is always well defined, since the only conditions required for GAG_A and FAF_A to define a valid Gaussian state are that GA = GA†G_A = G_A^\dagger and FA = −FAT F_A = -F_A^T, as dictated by the fermionic structure, that GAG_A has eigenvalues in [0,1][0,1], and that GA(1 −GA) ≥ FAFA†G_A(1 - G_A) \geq F_A F_A^\dagger , ensuring that the full correlation matrix CAC_A has eigenvalues in [0,1][0,1]. Therefore, it is always possible to remove the pairing term FAF_A without violating these constraints. By contrast, the opposite construction, namely defining a state with only pairing correlations (which would correspond to a state that completely breaks the symmetry, in the sense that its symmetrisation vanishes), is not possible. Our construction of Gaussian symmetrisation highlights an interesting structure: defining the three transformations as 𝒮 : ρ → ρQ\mathcal {S}:\rho \to \rho _Q, 𝒢 : ρ → ρC\mathcal {G}:\rho \to \rho _C and 𝒮(G) : ρC → ρC(s)\mathcal {S}^{(G)} : \rho _C \to \rho _{C^{(s)}}, it is clear that 𝒮\mathcal {S} and 𝒢\mathcal {G} do not commute. However we have that

𝒢(𝒮(ρ)) = 𝒮(G)(𝒢(ρ)) = 𝒢(𝒮(𝒢(ρ))). (2.8)\begin {equation} \mathcal {G(S(\rho ))} = \mathcal {S}^{(G)} (\mathcal {G}(\rho )) =\mathcal {G}( \mathcal {S} (\mathcal {G}(\rho )) ). \label {comp} \end {equation}

In the following sections we will show that this equation provides the natural way to define a fully Gaussian asymmetry measure.

3 Entanglement asymmetry in the Gaussian manifold

For simplicity of notation, since all states we consider from now on are Gaussian, we drop the nomenclature of the previous section, and we refer to a generic Gaussian state in AA as ρA\rho _A and to a symmetric state as ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)}. As highlighted in the above, given a state ρA\rho _A described by a correlation matrix (2.5) it is natural to consider a definition of symmetrisation at the level of the correlation matrix itself; this leads to a symmetrised state defined by the correlation matrix

CA(s) = ( GA 0 0 1 −GAT ) . (3.1)\begin {equation} C_A^{(s)} = \begin {pmatrix} G_A && 0\\0 && 1 -G_A^T \end {pmatrix}. \end {equation}

A natural quantifier of the “distance” between the two states is given by the relative entropy, as in the general definition of asymmetry [13],

ΔSA(G) = S(ρ A||ρA(s)) = Tr[ρ A(log⁡ρA −log⁡ρA(s))]. (3.2)\begin {equation} \Delta S^{(G)}_A = S(\rho _A||\rho _A^{(s)}) = \Tr [\rho _A (\log \rho _A - \log \rho _A^{(s)})]. \label {eq:gaussianasymmetry} \end {equation}

where the label (G)(G) stresses that this is a Gaussian definition of the entanglement asymmetry.

One of the main features of the standard EA is that it reduces to the difference of the entropies evaluated in the two states: we can show that the same is valid also in the present case, although this originates from a different mechanism. As discussed previously, the EH corresponding to ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} is quadratic and without pairing terms, log⁡ρA(s) ∼ ∑ x,yAx,ycx†cy\log \rho _A^{(s)} \sim \sum _{x,y} A_{x,y} c_x^\dag c_y, hence

Tr[ρA log⁡ρA(s)] ∼∑ x,yAx,y ⟨cx†c y⟩ ρA. (3.3)\begin {equation} \Tr [\rho _A \log \rho _A^{(s)}] \sim \sum _{x,y} A_{x,y} \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A}. \end {equation}

Since the two states ρA\rho _A and ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} share the normal component of the correlation matrix GAG_A, the two-point function is the same when evaluated over the two different density matrices, ⟨cx†cy⟩ ρA = ⟨cx†cy⟩ ρ A(s) \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A}= \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A^{(s)}} , implying immediately

Tr[ρA log⁡ρA(s)] = Tr[ρ A(s) log⁡ρ A(s)]. (3.4)\begin {equation} \Tr [\rho _A \log \rho _A^{(s)}]=\Tr [\rho _A^{(s)} \log \rho _A^{(s)}]. \end {equation}

Hence we conclude that the relative entropy simplifies to

ΔSA(G) = S(ρ A||ρA(s)) = S(ρ A(s)) −S(ρ A). (3.5)\begin {equation} \Delta S^{(G)}_A = S(\rho _A||\rho _A^{(s)}) = S(\rho _A^{(s)}) - S(\rho _A). \label {eq:separationofentropies} \end {equation}

Note that this result is exact, in contrast, for example, to the probe introduced in ref. [21], for which this property holds only approximately; we will however show that the two measures share some common features in the quench evolution. Since (3.5) only involves entropies of Gaussian states, it can be immediately evaluated through correlation matrix techniques [101],

S(CA) = −1 2Tr[CA log⁡CA + (1 −CA)log⁡(1 −CA)], (3.6)\begin {equation} \label {eq:entropyfromcorrelationmatrix} S(C_A) = -\frac {1}{2}\operatorname {Tr}[C_A \log C_A + (1-C_A) \log (1-C_A )], \end {equation}

which avoids completely the necessity of complicated integrals of charged moments as in the standard EA framework [13].

It is also possible to prove that ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} minimises the distance of ρA\rho _A from the manifold of symmetric Gaussian states, justifying its introduction as a good asymmetry measure. Consider a state σA\sigma _A which is symmetric and Gaussian, and is hence of the form σA = 1 Zσe−∑ x,yAxyσcx†cy \sigma _A = \frac {1}{Z_{\sigma }}e^{-\sum _{x,y} A^{\sigma }_{xy} c_x^\dag c_y}. We have

S(ρA||σA) = Tr[ρA(log⁡ρA −log⁡σA)] = ΔSA(G) + Tr[ρ A(log⁡ρA(s) −log⁡σ A)] (3.7) \begin {eqnarray} S(\rho _A||\sigma _A) &=& \Tr [\rho _A (\log \rho _A - \log \sigma _A)]\nonumber \\ &=&\Delta S^{(G)}_A + \Tr [\rho _A (\log \rho _A^{(s)}-\log \sigma _A )] \end {eqnarray}

Since both ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} and σA\sigma _A are symmetric Gaussian states, the expectation value of their logarithm is of the form

Tr[ρA(log⁡ρA(s) −log⁡σ A)] ∼∑ x,y (Axyρ −A xyσ) ⟨c x†c y⟩ ρA. (3.8)\begin {equation} \Tr [\rho _A (\log \rho _A^{(s)}-\log \sigma _A )] \sim \sum _{x,y}\left (A^{\rho }_{xy} -A_{xy}^{\sigma } \right ) \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A}. \end {equation}

Using again the fact that ρA\rho _A and ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} share the same normal part of the correlation matrix, we can apply the same simplification

∑ x,yAxyρ∕σ ⟨c x†c y⟩ ρA = ∑ x,yAxyρ∕σ ⟨c x†c y⟩ ρA(s) (3.9)\begin {equation} \sum _{x,y}A_{xy}^{\rho /\sigma } \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A} = \sum _{x,y}A_{xy}^{\rho /\sigma } \braket {c_x^\dag c_y}_{\rho _A^{(s)}} \end {equation}

allowing us to rewrite

Tr[ρA(log⁡ρA(s) −log⁡σ A)] = Tr[ρA(s)(log⁡ρ A(s) −log⁡σ A)]. (3.10)\begin {equation} \Tr [\rho _A (\log \rho _A^{(s)}-\log \sigma _A )] = \Tr [\rho _A^{(s)} (\log \rho _A^{(s)}-\log \sigma _A )]. \end {equation}

This is the relative entropy between the two symmetric states, which is non-negative and zero only if σA = ρA(s)\sigma _A = \rho _A^{(s)}, thus completing the proof. Hence, we conclude that ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A minimises the relative entropy of any Gaussian state with the manifold of symmetric Gaussian states. We stress that in general ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A is much larger than the standard asymmetry ΔSA\Delta S_A. In fact, while it is proven that ΔSA ≤ log⁡ℓA\Delta S_A \leq \log \ell _A [104], we will show in the following that the Gaussian asymmetry can be (and in general is) extensive in ℓA\ell _A. This property implies that the standard symmetrised state ρQ\rho _Q is in general very far from a Gaussian state.

Quench dynamics from generic states typically leads to symmetry restoration of the density matrix at long times. In standard asymmetry treatments, the symmetry restoration is understood as the statement ρA →t →∞ρA,Q\rho _{A} \overset {t\to \infty }{\to } \rho _{A,Q}. In the present context, since the evolution of ρA\rho _A takes place within the Gaussian manifold, this restoration is perhaps more properly understood as

ρA →t →∞ρA(s) (3.11)\begin {equation} \rho _A \overset {t\to \infty }{\to } \rho _A^{(s)} \end {equation}

Hence, the time evolution of ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A provides an alternative route to study dynamical symmetry restoration and its main features, including the Mpemba effect (which will be investigated in the next section). Note that there are also cases in which the state does not symmetrise, such as quenches from the tilted Néel state [78]; this effect can likewise be captured through the behavior of eq. (3.2). Moreover, using ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} instead of the standard symmetrisation enables the introduction of an alternative asymmetry quantifier based on the full counting statistics (FCS) of the charge, which we discuss in section 7.

As a final remark, we note that, beyond the significant analytical simplifications it offers, the Gaussian asymmetry admits a natural interpretation within the framework of quantum resource theory (QRT) [106, 107]. While the standard EA defines a monotone under general symmetric operations [10, 108, 109], its Gaussian counterpart defines a monotone under Gaussian symmetric operations. It therefore represents the appropriate measure when the dynamics is restricted to Gaussian operations, such as evolution under quadratic Hamiltonians, linear dissipative processes, or certain measurement protocols, including the quantum trajectories associated with continuous measurements of local fermionic densities.

4 Gaussian Mpemba effect

In this section, we show that the Gaussian asymmetry ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A in eq. (3.5) can be used to detect the Mpemba effect in the typical quench protocols considered in the literature. In particular, we demonstrate that ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A admits a simple description in terms of a quasiparticle picture, valid in the regime where both tt and ℓA\ell _A are large compared to microscopic scales, yet remain much smaller than the total system sise. We focus on the dynamics governed by hopping model H = −1 2 ∑ ici†ci+1 + h.c.H= -\frac {1}{2}\sum _i c_i^\dag c_{i+1} + \text {h.c.}, which corresponds to the fermionic formulation of the XX spin-chain, and consider as initial states a general class of coherent states frequently appearing in quench dynamics [92, 110], i.e.

|ψ⟩ ∝exp⁡ {∑ kM(k)ck†c −k†} |0⟩ (4.1)\begin {equation} \label {eq:coherent} \ket {\psi }\propto \exp \left \{\sum _k M(k) c_k^\dag c_{-k}^\dag \right \}\ket {0} \end {equation}

These states exhibit a pair structure by construction and therefore provide the simplest setting for applying the quasiparticle picture. Since in such quenches the normal part of the correlation matrix is time-independent,

⟨ck†(t)c q(t)⟩ = ⟨ck†c q⟩ = δk,q |M(k)|2 1 + |M(k)|2, (4.2)\begin {equation} \braket {c_k^\dag (t) c_q(t)} = \braket {c_k^\dag c_q } =\delta _{k,q} \frac {|M(k)|^2}{1+|M(k)|^2}, \label {eq:occ_coherent} \end {equation}

we immediately see that GAG_A does not evolve in time, and therefore ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} remains constant. On the other hand, the time-dependent phases in the pairing terms

⟨ck†(t)c q†(t)⟩ = δ q,−ke−2it𝜀k M(k) 1 + |M(k)|2, (4.3)\begin {equation} \braket {c_k^\dag (t) c^\dag _q(t)} =\delta _{q,-k} e^{-2it\varepsilon _k} \frac {M(k) }{1+|M(k)|^2}, \end {equation}

lead to cancellations in the real-space correlators ⟨cx†cy†⟩\braket {c_x^\dag c_y^\dag } via stationary phase cancellations, implying that FA(t) → 0F_A(t) \to 0 as t → ∞t\to \infty . Consequently, ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} is identical to lim t→∞ρA(t)\lim _{t \to \infty }\rho _A(t) and

S(ρA(s)) = S(ρ A(∞)). (4.4)\begin {equation} S(\rho _A^{(s)})=S(\rho _A(\infty )). \end {equation}

Hence S(ρA(s))S(\rho _A^{(s)}) corresponds to the volume law entropy arising in the long time limit in the quench dynamics of ρA\rho _A. Using that S(ρA)S(\rho _A) admits the quasiparticle formulation [81–83, 92]

S(ρA(t)) = ∫ dk 2πmin(2|vk|t,ℓA)sk(nk), (4.5)\begin {equation} S(\rho _A(t))= \int \frac {dk}{2\pi } \min (2|v_k|t,\ell _A) s_k(n_k), \label {eq:standard_qpp} \end {equation}

where sk = −(nk log⁡nk + (1 −nk)log⁡(1 −nk)))s_k = -\left (n_k\log n_k + (1-n_k) \log (1-n_k))\right ) is the pair contribution to the entanglement entropy, the Gaussian asymmetry is immediately obtained as

ΔSA(G) = ∫ dk 2πmax(ℓA − 2|vk|t,0)sk, (4.6)\begin {equation} \Delta S^{(G)}_A = \int \frac {dk}{2\pi } \max (\ell _A - 2|v_k|t,0) s_k, \label {eq:qpp_mpemba} \end {equation}

Eq. (4.6) provides a criterion for the onset of the Mpemba effect. Indeed, since sks_k is maximal when the occupation function is close to 1∕21/2 and vanishes when it approaches 00 or 11, and since the factor max(ℓA − 2|vk|t,0)\max (\ell _A - 2|v_k|t, 0) suppresses the contribution of fast modes first, it suffices to examine the relative purity of slow and fast modes. In particular, the Mpemba effect arises when the slow modes (with momenta k ∼ 0,πk \sim 0,\pi ) are “purer”, in the sense that their occupation functions are closer to 00 or 11, compared to the fast modes (with momenta k ∼ ±π∕2k \sim \pm \pi /2). This reasoning is in the same spirit as the discussion of ref. [20], in which the origin of the Mpemba effect was related to the amount of charge carried by fast and slow modes. In the present case, the charge carried by each excitation is precisely given by the occupation function, which uniquely determines the entanglement. This is a key feature of the quasiparticle picture: since entanglement is transported by particle-like excitations, its content is directly encoded in the occupation function. As discussed in refs. [110, 111], the occupation function can be interpreted as the single-particle charge eigenvalue associated with coarse-grained quasiparticle excitations, which are responsible for the propagation of entanglement.

5 Typical Gaussian asymmetry

Beyond the quantum Mpemba physics, key results obtained with entanglement asymmetry concern its characterisation in the context of random states [73–77], also in connection with black hole physics. A remarkable result is that the typical asymmetry of Haar random states exhibits a sharp discontinuity when the subsystem size reaches half of the total system size. It is then natural to investigate the typical properties of the Gaussian asymmetry ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A when averaging over the ensemble of Gaussian states. We are therefore interested in

where ⟨.⟩g \braket {.}_g represents the average over Gaussian states, see for instance [112]. This average can be accessed by following the approaches developed in refs. [112–115], since both ρA\rho _A and ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} are Gaussian.

It would be interesting to investigate whether the standard entanglement asymmetry for Gaussian random states also displays such smooth behavior; however, this lies beyond the scope of the present work.

6 Relation to entanglement asymmetry and non-Gaussianity

As emphasised throughout the previous sections, given a Gaussian state ρA\rho _A, its symmetrisation ρA,Q = ∑ qPqρAPq\rho _{A,Q} = \sum _q P_q \rho _A P_q is, in general, a strongly non-Gaussian state. This observation was one of the main motivations for introducing the Gaussian asymmetry ΔSA(G)\Delta S^{(G)}_A, which provides an asymmetry quantifier that remains entirely within the Gaussian manifold. Importantly, as discussed above, ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} can be viewed as the Gaussianisation of ρA,Q\rho _{A,Q}. This highlights a natural connection between the different asymmetry measures and the role of non-Gaussianity. In fact, recalling the definition of EA ΔS = S(ρQ) −S(ρ)\Delta S = S(\rho _Q) - S(\rho ) and the one of non-Gaussianity NG(ρQ) = S(ρQ(s)) −S(ρQ){\rm NG}(\rho _{Q})=S(\rho _Q^{(s)})-S(\rho _Q), and using eq. (2.8), the Gaussian asymmetry, can be rewritten as

ΔSA(G)(ρ A) = ΔSA(ρA) + NG(ρA,Q) (6.1)\begin {equation} \Delta S^{(G)}_A(\rho _A) = \Delta S_A(\rho _A) + {\rm NG}(\rho _{A,Q}) \label {eq:nongaussianity} \end {equation}

which implies NG(ρQ) = ΔSA(G) −ΔSA{\rm NG}(\rho _Q) = \Delta S^{(G)}_A - \Delta S_A, confirming the original insight that the difference between the two measures of asymmetry originates only from the non-Gaussianity of ρA,Q\rho _{A,Q}. For the second contribution, the symmetry of Φ\Phi implies that it commutes with symmetrisation, while its Gaussianity ensures that it commutes with Gaussianisation. NG(ρ~A,Q) = NG(Φ(ρA,Q)) ≤NG(ρA,Q), (6.2)\begin {equation} {\rm NG}(\tilde {\rho }_{A,Q}) = {\rm NG}\bigl (\Phi (\rho _{A,Q})\bigr ) \leq {\rm NG}(\rho _{A,Q}), \end {equation}

We conclude commenting on the case in which the state ρ\rho itself is not Gaussian. In this case, we first consider its Gaussianisation ρC\rho _C, then its Gaussian symmetrisation ρC(s)\rho _C^{(s)}. It is still natural to define

that through simple manipulations is

ΔSA(G)(ρ) = ΔS A(G)(ρ C) + NG(ρ), (6.4)\begin {equation} \Delta S^{(G)}_A (\rho ) = \Delta S^{(G)}_A(\rho _C) +{\rm NG}(\rho ), \end {equation}

Once again, the last equation expresses the idea that the difference between the two asymmetry measures quantifies the increase in non-Gaussianity NG(ρQ) −NG(ρ) {\rm NG}(\rho _Q) - {\rm NG}(\rho ) induced by the symmetrisation.

7 FCS Asymmetry in the Gaussian manifold

The behavior of the full counting statistics (FCS) of the charge QA = ∑ x∈Acx†cxQ_A = \sum _{x \in A} c_x^\dagger c_x in quenches from symmetric and non-symmetric Gaussian states (or integrable states in the interacting case) has been extensively analysed in refs. [8, 9, 116–123], and has also been experimentally measured [124]. In particular, these works have demonstrated that the FCS exhibits qualitatively distinct behavior depending on whether the initial state is symmetric or not, clearly highlighting the strong dependence of charge transport and fluctuation properties on symmetry.

Since the goal of asymmetry measures is to quantify the breaking of a symmetry in a given local state, it is natural to define a measure based on the difference between the two full counting statistics: that associated with ρA\rho _A and that associated with its symmetrised counterpart ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)}, namely

ΔZβ = log⁡Tr[eβQA ρA] −log⁡Tr[eβQA ρA(s)], β >0. (7.1)\begin {equation} \Delta Z_\beta = \log \operatorname {Tr}[e^{\beta Q_A} \rho _A] - \log \operatorname {Tr}[e^{\beta Q_A} \rho _A^{(s)}], \hspace {0.3cm}\beta >0 . \end {equation}

Note that the ordering is opposite to that used for the entropy, i.e., we subtract the symmetrised FCS from the original one. This choice reflects the intuitive expectation that charge fluctuations in a symmetric state are typically weaker than in a symmetry-breaking state, as will be made precise in the next section. It is important to stress that such a measure would not provide any information in the standard asymmetry scenario of ref. [13], since the FCS of the charge is identical when evaluated in ρA\rho _A and in ∑ qPqρAPq\sum _q P_q \rho _A P_q. However, this is no longer the case when considering ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)}. Indeed, this FCS-based measure captures differences between the two states at the level of the charge distribution, which is, in principle, directly accessible experimentally. In other words, the state can be distinguished from its Gaussian symmetrisation simply by analysing the statistics of the charge within the subsystem. We emphasise, however, that this quantity is not, strictly speaking, a proper measure of distance from the manifold of symmetric Gaussian states, as ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} does not generally minimise it. Rather, it should be viewed as a useful diagnostic that naturally emerges once it is recognised that ρA(s)\rho _A^{(s)} minimises the relative entropy.

8 Conclusions

We have introduced an asymmetry measure for Gaussian states that quantifies the degree of symmetry breaking without leaving the Gaussian manifold. This Gaussian asymmetry is defined as the relative entropy between a Gaussian state and its symmetrised counterpart, which can be shown to minimise the relative entropy over the set of symmetric Gaussian states, thereby defining a monotone under symmetric Gaussian operations. This framework allows one to analyse symmetry restoration using only correlation-matrix techniques and to derive simple analytical predictions for the dynamics in the ballistic regime via the quasiparticle picture. Furthermore, we have shown that this notion of symmetrisation enables the use of charge fluctuations as a diagnostic of symmetry restoration.

There are two natural directions for future work. First, it would be interesting to analyse more structured Gaussian protocols, including the presence of dissipation and measurements. While entanglement asymmetry has been partially investigated in such settings [30, 31, 42], the present quantifier is expected to provide a significantly simpler framework, enabling a more complete analytical understanding of symmetry restoration in these scenarios. A second ambitious direction concerns the extension of the present ideas to integrable quenches in interacting systems. In this context, integrable states play a role analogous to Gaussian states in free systems: they form a closed set under integrable dynamics and, with few exceptions, are the only states that allow for analytical control. It would therefore be natural to explore whether a notion of integrability-preserving symmetrisation can be defined. However, this is considerably more subtle than in the free case, as there is no obvious or canonical way to associate a symmetric integrable partner to a given initial integrable state.
